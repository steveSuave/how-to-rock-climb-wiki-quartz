## Why belay

A climber tied to a rope is only as safe as the person holding the other end. Belaying converts the rope and a [[belay-device]] into a one-way friction system: rope feeds out smoothly as the climber moves up; rope locks instantly under fall load.

## The belay site

Choose a site that is (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 161):
- **Safe from rockfall, dropped gear, falling climber**.
- **At the base of the rock** if possible — minimizes slack.
- **In line with the direction of pull** between the [[belay-anchor]] and the first piece of pro.

The **ABC rule**: **A**nchor – **B**elayer – **C**limber, in a straight line. Otherwise the impact will drag the belayer until they are.

## The mechanics

The belayer has two hands on the rope:
- **Guide hand** — handles the *live end* (the rope going to the climber). Feeds and senses tension.
- **Brake hand** — controls the *brake end*. Locks the rope to hold a fall. **The brake hand never leaves the rope.**

A leader fall: the brake hand quickly bends the rope to 45° away from the device, friction multiplies, the rope locks, the fall is held.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 160–161)

## Three-part take-in sequence

Used when bringing up a second on toprope or after each clip on lead (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 167–168):

1. **Pull** — guide hand pulls from above the device; brake hand pulls down from below.
2. **Pinch** — guide hand drops below the device and pinches both strands.
3. **Slide** — brake hand slides back to the device.

Repeat. Practice until automatic.

## Hip belay

Belaying without a device — rope around the waist, guide hand on the live end, brake hand on the brake end. Hip belays were standard until 1978 when belay devices took over (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 165–169).

Worth knowing in case you drop your belay device, but a device increases the margin of safety by orders of magnitude.

## Voice signals

The canonical set (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 221):

| Climber | Belayer |
| --- | --- |
| ON BELAY? | BELAY ON |
| CLIMBING | CLIMB |
| SLACK | (gives slack) |
| UP ROPE | (takes in slack) |
| TENSION / TAKE | (holds rope tight) |
| WATCH ME | (focuses attention) |
| FALLING! | (catches) |
| BELAY OFF | OFF BELAY! |
| ROCK! / ROPE! | (warning, both ways) |

## Direct vs. redirected belay

When the belayer is at the top of the pitch bringing up a second (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 233–234):
- **Redirected belay** — device on harness; rope goes up through a biner on the anchor, then down to the second. Doubles the load through the "pulley effect".
- **Direct belay** — device clipped *directly* to the anchor in autolock mode (Reverso, ATC Guide). The anchor takes the fall, not the belayer. Standard in Europe; preferred by US guides.

## Lowering

In [[sport-climbing]], the belayer almost always lowers the leader off the chains after she clips the top anchor. Critical: **never call "off belay"** if you intend to lower. The belayer keeps the rope locked and lowers slowly, two hands on the brake side, until the climber's feet are on the ground (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 275–276).

## Hanging belay

A belay with no ledge — the belayer hangs from the anchor. Common on big walls and overhanging routes. Tie in with enough slack to lean back. Belaying *through* the anchor (direct belay) is safer and more comfortable here (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 234–235).

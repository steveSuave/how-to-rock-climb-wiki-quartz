## The clean-climbing revolution

In the 1970s, climbers replaced [[pitons]] with chocks and nuts to avoid scarring the rock. Yvon Chouinard's Stoppers and Doug Robinson's "The Whole Natural Art of Protection" essay catalyzed the shift (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 116–117).

Modern passive pro is so light and works so well that even the most committed traditionalists carry only a token piton or two for emergencies.

## Tapers (Stoppers, RPs)

A wedge-shaped chock on a wire cable. The standard rack: a full set of wires from micro to ~1 inch.

| Profile | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **Straight taper** | Standard. Wedges in parallel-sided constrictions. |
| **Curved taper** | One side concave, one convex. Cams slightly as it loads — locks tighter. |
| **Offset taper** | Asymmetric (e.g., DMM Alloy Offset). Fits flaring cracks and pin scars. |
| **Micro-taper (RPs)** | Brass head soldered to wire. Holds 2–6 kN. Aid-climbing standard; trad emergency pro. |

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 119, 122–124)

The Black Diamond Stopper, rated at 10 kN (2,250 lbf), is the most common piece of trad protection. Black Diamond's QA manager has never seen a Stopper rated > 10 kN fail in 8 years of field reports (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 174).

## Tricams

A triangular-headed chock with a sling for activation. Wedges *or* cams depending on placement orientation. Particularly good in shallow pockets and horizontal cracks where neither a nut nor a cam fits (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 125–126).

## Hexes (Hexentrics)

Six-sided barrel-shaped chocks, slung with cord or sewn webbing. Can wedge in narrow cracks or *cam* in wider ones by rotating. Cheap and bomber but bulky and noisy. Largely supplanted by cams but still useful for big offwidth-ish sizes (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 126–128).

## Big Bros

Telescoping spring-loaded tubes by Trango — protection for **off-widths** that are too wide for cams. Sizes from 4" to 12". Heavy and bulky but the only viable pro for wide cracks (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 128–129).

## Placing nuts: the SOS check

Tom Cecil's mnemonic for every placement (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 140):

- **S**olid — the rock around the placement is good (no flakes, no detached blocks).
- **O**rientation — the piece is oriented to take the [[direction-of-pull]] from a fall.
- **S**urface area — maximum metal-to-rock contact.

A well-placed nut:
1. Settles into a constriction with a firm downward tug.
2. Has the wire pointing in the expected direction of pull.
3. Is extended with a [[carabiners|quickdraw]] or sling so rope drag doesn't lift it out.

## Removal

A **nut tool** — a flat metal hook on a wire handle — pries stuck nuts loose by tapping them upward and out of the constriction (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 218).

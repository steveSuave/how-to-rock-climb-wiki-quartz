## Definition

**Fall factor** = length of fall ÷ length of rope paid out.

A **factor 2 fall** occurs when the leader falls *twice the rope length above the anchor* — possible only when there's no protection between the leader and the [[belay-anchor]]. The lab-simulated factor 2 drop test (UIAA standard) drops an 80 kg weight 5 m on 2.5 m of rope (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 173).

## Why it's the worst

Force on the anchor depends on the fall factor, *not* the fall distance. A factor 2 fall on 5 m of rope generates the same impact force as a factor 2 fall on 50 m — because the same fraction of the rope is stretching to absorb the same energy.

A factor 2 fall lab-test generates ~12 kN (2,640 lbf) on the rope and through the system. This is the UIAA-tested maximum impact a rope is allowed to transmit (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 91).

## Real-world vs. lab

In the lab, the test rope is shock-loaded with an iron weight — no belayer, no harness, no rope slip, no body distortion. In reality, all of these absorb energy (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 173):

| Energy absorber | Estimated reduction |
| --- | --- |
| Climber's body and harness | 5–10% |
| Belayer being lifted | 0–10% |
| Belay device rope slippage | 5–10% |
| Rope stretch in the full rope length | 20–40% |

The book's revised estimate (per Craig Connally's *Mountaineering Handbook*): a real-world factor 2 fall puts ~1,900 lbf on the topmost piece — or as low as 1,520 lbf with a human (not iron) climber (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 175).

## The Golden Rule

> An anchor system is not good enough unless it can withstand the greatest force that can possibly be put to it, known as a factor 2 fall.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 173)

## How to avoid one

The book's prescription:

1. **Place the [[jesus-nut]] *immediately* off the belay.** Don't run it out 20 ft to "find a better placement."
2. If a good first placement isn't available, *double up* — two pieces equalized.
3. Anchor the belayer for *upward* pull (the belayer is the counterweight).
4. Build the [[belay-anchor]] [[srene|SRENE]] — assume the worst case.

## Why the leader still falls onto pro, not the anchor

When the [[jesus-nut]] holds, the fall force is transmitted through the rope and over that piece, which acts as a redirect. The top piece bears the sum of both rope strands — roughly twice the load on the belayer/anchor side. So the anchor sees about *half* the load that the Jesus nut sees, and far less than a direct factor-2 hit (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 175).

Only when the Jesus nut blows does the belay anchor take a direct factor 2 fall. Hence the Jesus nut is the most important placement on the entire pitch.

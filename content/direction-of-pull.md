## What determines direction of pull

Two cases:

### Leading
The leader falls. The rope comes taut between the belayer and the highest piece of [[passive-protection|pro]] above the [[belay-anchor]]. The anchor is pulled *toward* that piece.

- If the leader is straight above with one piece in, pull is straight up.
- If the leader has traversed left after one piece, pull is up-and-to-the-left until the leader places a second piece, then pull is toward the new piece.

### Following
The follower falls. The rope comes taut between the belayer and the *last* piece of pro below — the one the follower is approaching. As the follower removes pieces, the pull point migrates *up* the route toward where the follower is.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 177–179)

## Why it matters

A [[passive-protection|nut]] is designed to hold *down*. A cam placed for a downward pull will be ineffective — or fail — if loaded sideways or upward. The anchor's pieces must be set with their own direction of pull *aligned* with the anchor's expected loading direction (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 184).

When the route's [[direction-of-pull]] is uncertain (e.g., a face climb that wanders), build a **multidirectional anchor**: pieces oriented to resist pulls from multiple directions, or use a [[rigging-systems|self-equalizing system]] (sliding X, equalette, quad) that re-orients to whatever direction the load comes from.

## Swing

When a leader traverses left and falls, the leader's fall path is an arc — a "swing" — that pulls the anchor across a 90° (or more) range as the leader swings into the fall line. The anchor must be built to sustain the *entire arc* (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 182).

Recent anchor failures, the book notes, were caused by swing — climbers built anchors for the *static* loading direction but didn't account for the swing arc. *"Bottom line: When a swinging fall is possible, any anchor that is not fashioned to withstand loading across the full arc of the swing and a few degrees beyond is not good enough."*

## Judging direction of pull

To predict direction of pull:

1. **Know where the route goes** — study the topo, reference fixed gear, look up.
2. **Find the [[jesus-nut]] location** — that's the first piece off the belay, and where the rope will run from.
3. **Extrapolate the rope path** to the next likely placements.

You won't know exactly until the leader has placed the Jesus nut. But you can usually be within 5° (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 178–179).

## Angle of the dangle

When two primary placements are connected with a sling at the power point, they form a V. The wider the V's angle, the more *force on each placement* — known as **load multiplication**:

| V angle at power point | Force per placement (for 100 lb load) |
| --- | --- |
| 20° | 50 lb |
| 40° | 54 lb |
| 80° | 70 lb |
| 120° | 100 lb |
| 170° | ~600 lb |

**Rule of thumb: keep the V angle below 60°.** Decrease angles by extending the power point lower with longer slings or a [[cordelette]] (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 183).

## Upward oppositionals

When a leader fall could lift the belayer, an upward-pulling piece keeps the belayer in place. Required (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 211):
- Belayer significantly lighter than the leader.
- Belaying below an overhang with [[jesus-nut]] above or behind.
- Lift potential > 18".

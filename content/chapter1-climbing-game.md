## What rock climbing is

Rock climbing is one of several climbing games — alongside [[bouldering]], [[sport-climbing]], aid climbing, big-wall and alpine mountaineering — that share a common skill base: rope management, physical movement, mental conditioning (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 303). What distinguishes pure rock climbing is that the focus is on technical movement up a roped vertical pitch, not on summiting a peak.

## Short history

- **Late 1800s, Alps**: roped climbing on rock was practiced as an offshoot of alpinism, called *varappe* (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 3). Rope existed only as a safety backup; "fair play" meant each climber was expected to climb his own peak.
- **1950s–60s, Yosemite Valley**: free climbing standards rose in tandem with aid climbing as climbers pushed the granite walls of El Capitan, Half Dome, and the Cathedral Rocks. John Salathé, Royal Robbins, Yvon Chouinard, and others established modern American trad climbing (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 242).
- **1970s**: the clean-climbing movement replaced [[pitons]] with [[passive-protection]] (Stoppers, Hexentrics) to spare the rock.
- **1980s**: European [[sport-climbing]] crosses the Atlantic. Bolted faces, hangdogging, and redpoint ascents reshape the ethic.
- **1986 onward**: indoor [[climbing-gym]]s and gymnastic [[training]] (campus boards, hangboards) drive a step-change in standards.

## The Yosemite Decimal System (YDS)

Routes are classified by terrain class (1–6) and, within Class 5, by a difficulty decimal (5.0–5.15) with a/b/c/d subgrades above 5.10. See [[yosemite-decimal-system]] for the full breakdown.

A route also carries a Grade I–VI (time/seriousness) and may carry an R or X rating for runout/death-fall potential (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 5–8).

## Required reading mindset

The book stresses that climbing is *inherently dangerous*; no manual can substitute for an experienced mentor (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. xiii). The thread that runs through all ten chapters is John Dill's safety triad — ignorance, casualness, and distraction — explored in [[chapter10-responsibilities]].

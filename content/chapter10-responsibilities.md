## Stewardship and access

Climbers carry trash out, dispose of human waste away from cliffs and trails, respect nesting birds, stay on established access trails, and don't sue landowners after an accident. Access closures spread like wildfire when climbers behave badly; the **Access Fund** (P.O. Box 17010, Boulder, CO 80308; accessfund.org) lobbies and finances to keep crags open (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 300).

## Etiquette

The first party at the base gets the route. Offer to let a faster party pass when convenient, but never out of obligation if you're uncomfortable having someone above you. If parties are waiting, finish and move on — don't camp on a popular line (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 300).

## John Dill's three causes

From the Yosemite head rescue ranger John Dill's safety essay, distilled from 35+ years of accident investigation (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 300):

> State of mind is the key to safety.

Mental lapses fall into three categories:

### Ignorance
"I thought it never rained..." Fight ignorance two ways:
- **Read** — back issues of climbing magazines, *Accidents in North American Mountaineering* (yearly compilation of case reports).
- **Practice** — book learning alone gives complacency. Several climbers have waited until midnight in a rainstorm to learn how to Prusik for the first time.

### Casualness
"I just didn't take it seriously." Sub-categories:
- **Habit reinforcement** — getting away with risky shortcuts breeds laziness.
- **Reinforcement by others' experiences/states** — junior climbers ape veterans' casual attitude on routes the veterans have wired.
- **Memory decay** — "I'll never go up again without rain gear!" forgotten by next month.
- **Civilization** — fixed anchors and chalk hide how dangerous the climbing actually is.
- **Speed myth** — "a fast rescue makes accidents acceptable." It doesn't.
- **Overconfidence** — "I'll never have this happen to me. I'm a safe, cautious climber." Many killed climbers were described this way.

### Distraction
Fear, thirst, sore feet, summit fever, fatigue, hurry. Two case patterns:
- Experienced climbers making "beginner errors" on day climbs because their minds were already at the post-climb bivouac.
- Darkness once caught two climbers — they rushed, panicked, argued; one was killed rappelling off the end of his rope.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 301)

## The other climbing games

The book closes by zooming out — rock climbing is one of many games (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 303):
- **Big walls** — multi-day pure rock on monoliths like El Capitan, Hidden Tower (Pakistan).
- **Mountaineering** — riskiest; storms, avalanches, crevasses, cold and altitude.
- **Ice climbing**, sea cliffs, desert, limestone, gym, sport, snow, ski, peak-bagging, soloing.

The competent rock climber has the rope, movement, and mental fundamentals that transfer to any mode of ascent.

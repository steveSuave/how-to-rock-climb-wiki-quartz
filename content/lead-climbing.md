## What leading is

The leader sets off from the [[belay-anchor]] with the lead end of the [[rope]], clipping the rope to protection as she ascends. The belayer pays out rope through a [[belay-device]] below. If the leader falls, the protection holds — provided it was placed well and oriented to the [[direction-of-pull]].

Two flavors:
- **[[sport-climbing|Sport leading]]** — clipping pre-placed [[bolts]] with [[carabiners|quickdraws]].
- **Trad leading** — placing [[passive-protection]] and [[camming-devices]] as one goes.

## The rack

The collection of gear carried on the lead. A catchall trad rack (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 218):

- Full set of wired [[passive-protection|nuts]] from micro to ~1 inch
- Progressively sized [[camming-devices|cams]] from finger-size to 2–3"
- 5–6 shoulder-length runners
- 10 quickdraws
- Locking biners for anchors
- Nut tool

Racked on harness gear loops (preferred) or a shoulder sling (for wide-crack routes). Sorted by size, gates facing the body. **Standardize the racking method** so the hand finds gear without looking (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 220).

## Voice signals

See [[chapter6-art-of-leading]] for the full list. The basics:
- ON BELAY? / BELAY ON
- CLIMBING / CLIMB
- SLACK / UP ROPE
- TENSION / TAKE
- FALLING!
- BELAY OFF / OFF BELAY
- ROCK!

## Protection and direction of pull

The leader must continuously assess which way each piece below will be loaded if she falls. Long runners extend off-line placements so the rope follows a straight plumb line — minimizing both rope drag and the sideways force that can pop pieces (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 222–224).

The first piece off the deck must be **multidirectional** — solid against an upward yank as well as a downward pull (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 224).

## When to place pro

There is no rule. Experienced leaders place pro when they need it; beginners place often, especially before a [[crack-climbing|crux]]. Rules of thumb (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 225):

- Anticipate the crux; place gear at the *start* of any hard section.
- Don't run it out more than ~10 ft for a beginner.
- Never let a fall put you in reach of a ledge or feature you'd hit.
- Place gear on rest stances, not while burning out on the crux move.

## Cleaning and changing leads

The follower **cleans** the route by removing each piece of pro as she climbs through. At the top, she clips into the anchor with two biners *before* coming off belay. The leader unties and re-racks; the next pitch begins (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 234–236).

## Double-rope technique

Two thin (8.5–9 mm) ropes clipped *alternately* into pro reduce rope drag on wandering routes and provide a built-in rappel line. Largely European; rare in the US (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 236–238). See [[chapter6-art-of-leading]].

## Falling

The fear of falling is the main mental barrier on lead. The fact is that a leader fall on bomber [[passive-protection|pro]] is *not inherently dangerous*. See [[falling]].

## The first lead

The book recommends:
1. Take a climbing course; learn belaying, lowering, gym leading first.
2. First trad lead: 5.3–5.4, with a [[toprope]] backup if possible.
3. Aim for a *direct, easy* climb — no shenanigans.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 215–216)

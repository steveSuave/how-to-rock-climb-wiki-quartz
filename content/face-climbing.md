## Types of face

| Angle | Style |
| --- | --- |
| Slab (<vertical) | Friction, smearing, weight over feet |
| Vertical | Edging on positive holds, body in balance |
| Overhanging (>vertical) | Powerful, dynamic; backstep and drop knee keep hips close to rock |

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 38–43)

## Slab climbing

Hold the **tripod**: feet wide apart, butt out, arms relaxed. Friction is generated by *body weight pressing through the rubber*, not by gripping with hands. The hardest slab routes are 5.13+ but feel utterly committing because the protection is sparse and falls are skidding (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 38).

## Vertical face

Three points of contact. Move one limb at a time. Use [[footwork]] to keep weight on the legs; the [[handholds]] are for balance and the occasional pull.

The "[[footwork|backstep]]" — outside edge on a hold while the hip turns to the wall — is the workhorse position on vertical climbing.

## Overhanging face

Steep climbing punishes inefficient body position. Key techniques:

- **Backstep** and **drop knee** — turn the hip into the wall to bring the center of mass close.
- **Flagging** — let the free leg dangle out for counterweight.
- **Heel hook** — pull yourself in with a foot above the waist.
- **Toe hook** — top of foot under an undercling, pulling away from the wall.
- **Move from the core** — hips drive the climbing; arms transmit force the core has already set up (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 47).

When holds are too far apart for static reach, use [[dynamic-climbing]].

## Looking ahead

Plot a 3–4 move sequence from each rest. Lock in the rest hand, scan, commit. A face climber's strategy is to break a difficult section into discrete body lengths between rest stops (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 231).

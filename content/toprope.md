## What it is

A [[rope]] runs from the climber's harness *up* through an anchor at the top of the climb and *back down* to a belayer on the ground. The belayer takes in rope as the climber ascends. If the climber falls, the protection has already been engineered above — the fall is limited by [[rope]] stretch only.

Toprope is the safest form of roped climbing and is the universal entry point for new climbers.

## Setting a toprope

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 287–289)

1. Walk or scramble to the top.
2. **Belay yourself in** so you can work near the lip safely — most rigging is done while leaning toward the cliff.
3. Build a [[belay-anchor]] using a hulking [[natural-anchors|tree]], a boulder, or [[bolts|bolt anchors]] if available.
4. **Extend the anchor** over the lip with 20–30 ft of static line, slings, or a 25 ft cordelette. The rope should run *just over* the lip — not down behind a sharp edge.
5. Clip two locking biners (opposed) at the bottom of the anchor.
6. Run the rope through both biners.
7. Throw the ends down.

## Common pitfalls

- **Anchor too far back from the lip** — the rope rubs the lip, abrading the sheath. A single lowering can wear through to the core (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 289).
- **Anchor too close / not extended** — the rope kinks over the lip.
- **Single bolt** — never.
- **Aluminum rap rings** — never lower or top-rope through.
- **Loose rock at the top** that the lowering rope can knock off onto people below.

## The belayer

If the climber heavily outweighs the belayer (> 20 lb), anchor the belayer to the ground. A swinging toprope fall can lift a lighter belayer 5–20 feet into the air (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 289).

Use a [[belay-device]] with a locking biner clipped to the belay loop. Two hands on the brake side.

## When the climber's path wanders

On a wandering or traversing toprope, falls can swing the climber far from the line. **Directionals** — cams or nuts placed on the rope to keep it in line — limit the swing (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 289). The toprope leader places them on rappel; the climber unclips and re-clips as she passes.

## Stretch

A toprope fall is just the rope stretch. A 50 ft climb with a little slack can produce a 5+ ft fall on stretchy lead rope. Static lines (no stretch) are preferred for dedicated toprope crags where one rope serves dozens of laps per day (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 289–290).

## Use cases

- **Beginners** — the standard first climbing experience.
- **Sport-route rehearsal** — wire a hard route before redpointing.
- **Training** — high volume of vertical without the mental load of leading.
- **Photography / instruction** — secure and stable platform for the team to work.

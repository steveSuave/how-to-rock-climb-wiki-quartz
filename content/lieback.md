## When to lieback

A lieback works where the crack offers a positive edge and a facing wall to push against — typically:
- **Flake cracks** — a flake of rock pulled away from the wall.
- **Corner / dihedral cracks** — where a crack meets a 90° wall.

It's the fastest way to ascend such terrain but among the most strenuous (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 76–80).

## Mechanics

1. Hands wrap the edge of the crack, palms in, thumbs down.
2. Body weight hangs back, away from the wall.
3. Feet step *up and across*, pressing against the opposing face.
4. The pull of the hands counter-balances the push of the feet.
5. Walk the hands and feet up alternately, like climbing a rope ladder sideways.

The amount of body lean is critical:
- **Too vertical** — feet skid off.
- **Too horizontal** — hands burn out from over-pulling.

A "Frankenstein" lean (slight lean back, fairly upright) is the rest position.

## The protection problem

Protection in a lieback crack must be placed *while leaning back*. Climbers often have to:
- Stop the lieback,
- Plug a hand or finger jam (essentially abandoning the lieback to face mode),
- Place the gear,
- Resume the lieback.

This stop-and-go is what makes a long lieback exhausting. Camming devices help — they go in fast — but the crack must be a width that takes cams.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 76–80)

## Lieback vs. jam

Many cracks can be either liebacked *or* jammed. The decision depends on:
- **Strength reserves** — jamming is slower but burns less arm.
- **Protection** — jamming offers easier pro placement.
- **Hold quality** — a flaring crack may not take jams but takes a lieback.

Veterans switch between the two on the same pitch, choosing whichever rests their tired hand at the moment.

## What makes off-widths brutal

Conventional [[jamming]] doesn't work — the gap is too wide for a fist, too narrow for the body. Progress relies on arm-and-leg cams against opposing walls, inching upward by alternating which limb takes weight. It's tedious, awkward, and grueling (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 65–66).

## Arm-and-shoulder camming

### Arm bar
Forearm and palm against one wall, shoulder/elbow against the opposite wall. The arm is straight, locked in compression. The hand may also press up against an internal feature.

### Chicken wing (arm lock)
The arm is bent — elbow into one wall, palm/forearm into the other. Stronger than an arm bar for narrow off-widths but harder on the shoulder (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 67–68).

## Leg camming

### Heel-toe
Foot perpendicular to the crack: heel pressed against one wall, toe against the other. Standard footwork in off-widths.

### Knee lock (kneebar)
Knee bent and cammed against one wall, foot pressed against the other. The thigh becomes a third limb. Rest stance.

### T-bar foot stack
Both feet stacked perpendicular to the crack — heel of one against toe of the other, like a T — to span a width wider than a heel-toe.

## Hand stacking (Leavittation)

Named after Randy Leavitt. Both hands stacked thumb-to-thumb across the crack, palms outward, providing a wide compression cam. Used in chest-width to chimney-narrow cracks where no single hand or fist will fit (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 69).

## The off-width sequence

1. Insert the inside limb (arm bar / chicken wing).
2. Stack the feet (heel-toe or T-bar).
3. Push up with the legs while the arm holds position.
4. Reset the arm a few inches higher.
5. Walk the feet up.
6. Repeat.

Progress is *measured in inches per minute*.

## Gear

[[passive-protection|Big Bros]] (expandable tubes) and large [[camming-devices]] (#5, #6 Camalot) are the only pro for off-widths. Set them every few feet and hope they don't walk.

## Why bother

The book's author notes off-widths are "rarely esthetic, rarely fun, but always character-building." Wide cracks are also where the bulk of unprotected runouts live in classic American trad, so the technique is unavoidable on long routes (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 65–70).

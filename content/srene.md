## The acronym

| Letter | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **S**olid | Each primary placement is itself bombproof — set in good rock, properly oriented. |
| **R**edundant | The system has no single point of failure: 3+ pieces, multiple slings, locking biners (or opposed pairs). |
| **E**qualized | Load is distributed across the placements, ideally evenly. |
| **N**o **E**xtension | If one placement fails, the remaining anchor catches the load without dropping the climber a noticeable distance. |

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 186–187)

## What SRENE is and isn't

**Is**: an *evaluation strategy* for an anchor — a checklist to ask before tying in.

**Isn't**: a perfect anchor specification. The book is clear: no anchor is ever perfectly equalized, and some redundant systems involve compromise (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 186).

> SRENE is a good code to follow, but there are anchors out there that do not conform to SRENE, yet they are literally capable of holding a car.

The SRENE checklist is a discipline, not a recipe.

## Solid

The *individual placements* must hold (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 185):
- Rock around the nut is solid (no flakes).
- Cam lobes contact rock evenly.
- Bolt hangers are flush and not loose.
- Tree is alive, ≥ 6" diameter, well-rooted.

No rigging trick can rescue bad primary placements.

## Redundant

Boeing 747s do not have two left wings. They have *one* wing built to a far higher standard than a doubled-up wing. The same is true of climbing rope and harness — single elements engineered to a strict standard.

But climbing anchors are improvised from gear in unknown rock. So redundancy is the safety net:
- 3+ primary placements.
- Multiple connection elements (sling, cord) between placements and power point.
- Two locking biners (or one locker plus opposed pair) at the power point.

NASA failure-rate math (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 188):
- One leg, failure rate 1/1,000.
- Two equal legs, 1/1,000,000.
- Three legs, slight further improvement.
- Four+ legs, negligible improvement.

So **doubling up** is the decisive jump in security; tripling and beyond is marginal but worth the small effort.

## Equalized

Load distributed across primaries. **Perfect equalization is impossible** in practice — even between two identical bolts, one always loads first because of cord stretch differences (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 198–199).

The [[rigging-systems|cordelette]] is a *statically equalized* system: it distributes load only in one anticipated direction. The **sliding X**, **equalette**, and **quad** are *automatically equalized*: they self-adjust when the load direction changes.

In real anchors, equalization is a relative term — distribution rather than equalization.

## No Extension

If one placement fails, the remaining system should:
- Take the load without dropping the climber more than a few inches.
- Not shock-load the remaining placements catastrophically.

The **sliding X** offers automatic equalization but at the cost of extension if one piece fails. **Limiter knots** in the sliding X reduce this (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 200).

Recent testing suggests short extensions are not as severe or dangerous as once feared, but more tests are needed (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 187).

## When SRENE conflicts

In real climbing, the four criteria sometimes contradict:
- Equalization with no extension is hard with > 2 pieces.
- A multidirectional anchor is harder to equalize than a single-direction one.
- A redundant anchor in soft rock may require spreading the placements out, breaking equalization.

The book's bottom line: **understand the principles, then improvise**. Anchor building is a lifelong study (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 188).

## The Golden Rule

> An anchor system is not good enough unless it can withstand the greatest force that can possibly be put to it, known as a factor 2 fall.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 173)

A factor 2 fall is what happens when a leader falls before placing the first piece off the belay — the whole fall force lands on the anchor with no intervening protection to absorb it. UIAA lab tests of a factor 2 generate ~12 kN; real-world peak forces are lower because the belayer's body, rope slip, and harness distortion all absorb energy (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 173, 175).

## The Jesus nut

The first piece of protection above the belay — the [[jesus-nut]] — is the most critical placement on the entire pitch. If it holds, the belay never sees a factor 2 (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 175–176).

> If it fails, the next person you see will be Jesus.

## SRENE

Marc Chauvin's acronym for anchor evaluation: **S**olid, **R**edundant, **E**qualized, **N**o **E**xtension. SRENE is an evaluation strategy, not an absolute — most anchors compromise on one axis (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 186). See [[srene]].

## Direction of pull

Every fall pulls on the anchor from a *specific direction* — a direct line between the belayer and the first piece above (when leading) or the last piece below (when seconding). When the leader traverses, swing turns the anchor load into an arc; build the anchor multidirectional (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 177–182). See [[direction-of-pull]].

## Angle of the dangle

Connecting two primary anchors with a sling forms a V at the power point. The wider the V, the more force on each placement — known as *load multiplication*. Keep the V angle below 60° (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 183).

## Rigging systems

Four modern rigging methods, in order from static to self-equalizing:
- **[[cordelette|Cordelette]]** — a 7 mm nylon or 5.5 mm high-tensile loop, tied off with a figure-eight at a single power point. Best for two equal-length placements; less effective when arms are unequal.
- **Sliding X** — a twisted sling on two placements that self-adjusts as the load shifts. Limit knots prevent excessive extension on failure.
- **Equalette** — a doubled cord with two limiter knots; equalizes 3–4 placements better than a cordelette and accommodates off-axis loading.
- **Quad** — a doubled equalette; near-perfect equalization for two side-by-side bolts. Most common at modern sport top-anchors.

See [[rigging-systems]].

## Step-by-step belay anchor

1. Determine direction(s) of pull for both this pitch's follower and the next pitch's leader.
2. Locate a stance with adequate primary placements.
3. Place the strongest, most bombproof piece first (multidirectional if possible).
4. Shore up with secondary placements close together, but spread across features if rock is suspect.
5. Connect with a rigging system to safeguard the full arc.
6. Tie in via figure-8-on-a-bight or clove hitch to the power point.
7. ABC: Anchor-Belayer-Climber, in a line.
8. KISS: Keep It Simple, Stupid.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 191)

## Upward oppositionals

When the belayer is much lighter than the leader, or below an overhang with the Jesus nut directly above, an upward-pulling piece prevents the belayer from being yanked off the stance and lifted more than ~18 inches (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 211).

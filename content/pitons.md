## History

Until the 1970s, pitons were the primary form of [[lead-climbing]] protection — steel spikes hammered into the rock with a piton hammer. The repeated hammering and prying widened cracks at popular routes and scarred the rock (the "Chouinard scars" on Yosemite cracks). Yvon Chouinard and Tom Frost led the clean-climbing movement to replace pitons with chocks (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 141–143).

Today most US free climbing is piton-free. Pitons survive in:
- **Big-wall aid climbing** — where blank rock dictates aid.
- **Fixed pins** — pre-placed in popular trad routes; never removed.
- **Alpine retreat** — a single piton may be the only emergency anchor.

## Types

| Piton | Width | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **RURP** (Realized Ultimate Reality Piton) | < 1/4" | Tiny hooked blade for hairline cracks. Aid only. Holds body weight, maybe. |
| **Birdbeak** | < 1/4" | Hooked blade with a hammer-set point. Aid only. |
| **Knifeblade** | 1/4" – 3/8" | Thin blade for thin cracks. Body weight to short fall. |
| **Lost Arrow** | 1/2" | Square-cut blade. Bomber when well-set. |
| **Bugaboo** | ~1" | Mid-size. Largely replaced by hexes / cams. |
| **Angle** | 3/4" – 1.5" | V-cross-section. Springs into cam-like grip in the crack. |
| **Bong (bong-bong)** | 2" – 4" | Wide-mouthed angle. Almost extinct — large [[passive-protection|hexes]] and [[camming-devices|big cams]] do the job. |
| **Bashie** | Variable | Malleable metal hammered into a seam. Aid only. |

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 143; Appendix B)

## Placing a piton

1. Find a crack a hair narrower than the piton width.
2. Insert the piton until it stops by hand.
3. Hammer with progressively harder blows.
4. Listen for the "ringing" pitch — a good placement rises in pitch as it seats. A dull thud means the rock is fractured or the placement is bottoming out (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 143–144).
5. Sling with the eye, not the body.

A piton that pulls out is more dangerous than no piton — the climber assumed it would hold.

## Fixed pitons

A piton hammered in years or decades ago and left in place. Often the only available anchor on classic routes. **Inspect every fixed pin** (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 145):

- Visible rust pitting → suspect.
- Wiggles when tugged → bad.
- The eye is bent or worn → marginal.
- The placement looks pristine (recent) → still test it.

If in doubt, back it up with a nut. Never remove a fixed pin — generations of climbers depend on it.

## Why pitons aren't used today

- Damage the rock irreversibly.
- Heavier than passive pro.
- Slower than cams.
- Cause access conflicts (chalk and scars draw landowner ire).

## Definition

Sport climbing is climbing where the protection is *exclusively bolts*. The leader carries shoes, harness, rope, and ~10–15 [[carabiners|quickdraws]] — no rack of nuts or cams. Most sport routes are ≤100 ft (a single rope length); the leader lowers off chained anchors back to the ground (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 273).

By engineering the hazards out, sport climbing lets the climber focus on the moves. The trade-off: sport routes are typically steep to overhanging, and falls are quick and hard.

## Origin

Sport climbing came from Europe in the 1980s — first the limestone of France and Spain, then imported into the US (Smith Rock, Oregon was an early hot spot). The bolt-protected, gymnastic style was initially controversial in the US trad community; by the mid-1990s it had become the dominant form of free climbing (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 5, 273).

## The setup

- **Rack**: 10–15 quickdraws on a gear loop, two locking biners for the anchor, a 60-cm sling, a [[belay-device]].
- **Rope**: 60 m or 70 m, 9.2–9.9 mm.
- **Belay**: with a [[belay-device]] (Grigri popular for projecting), belayer anchored if much lighter than the leader.

## Cleaning the chains

The standard sequence at the top anchor (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 276):

1. Clip into both anchor bolts with a sling and locker (or two opposed regular biners).
2. Pull up a bight, tie a figure-eight, clip into your belay loop with a locker.
3. Untie the original tie-in knot.
4. Thread the rope through the chains (or rappel rings).
5. Retie a figure-eight follow-through to your harness.
6. Backup biner stays clipped while you check everything.
7. Dismantle the sling, weight the rope, call "TENSION" / "LOWER ME".
8. Belayer lowers slowly.

## Communication

The #1 sport-climbing accident pattern (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 274):

- Leader calls "OFF BELAY" at the chains, intending to lower.
- Belayer takes the climber off.
- Leader leans back to lower.
- Decks.

**Never call "off belay" if you intend to lower.** The signal for lowering is "LOWER" or "TAKE" or a thumbs-down.

## Lowering

Belayer two hands on the brake side; smooth, steady, not too fast or too slow. Slow lowering builds friction on the anchor that wears the sheath (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 275).

**Never** lower with the rope running through:
- Nylon slings (friction burns).
- Cord (friction burns).
- Aluminum rap rings (friction wear).

Lower *only* through steel chain links, welded cold shuts, or [[carabiners|biners]].

## Cleaning the route

The lower-off leader removes each quickdraw as she descends. On overhanging routes, **tramming** — clipping into the loaded rope strand on each draw — keeps the cleaner from swinging out of reach (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 278).

## Style

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 279)

- **Hangdog** — hang on each bolt to rehearse moves. Rehearsal mode.
- **Redpoint** — clean lead from the ground, no hangs, no falls.
- **Pinkpoint** — clean lead with quickdraws pre-placed on the bolts.
- **On-sight flash** — first try, no prior knowledge, no falls.
- **Flash** — first try with prior beta.

## Training advice

The book's brief sport-training program (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 280–281):

1. Warm up on an easy route — always.
2. Climb 6–10 routes per day, spanning doable / near-limit / at-limit / beyond.
3. Two to three climbing days per week (one indoors).
4. Avoid running laps on routes you have wired.
5. The real 5.12 threshold is **5.11c**.

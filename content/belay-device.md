## Why a belay device

A belay device creates enough friction that a single-handed belayer can hold any conceivable fall load. Without one, the [[belay|hip belay]] is the alternative — workable, but minimal margin.

## Device classes

### Tube (ATC-style)
The Black Diamond **ATC** ("Air Traffic Controller") is the prototype. A bight of rope is pulled through a slot and clipped through a locking biner. Friction comes from the rope bending around the slot. Variants:
- **ATC-XP** — modified ATC for thinner ropes.
- **ATC Guide** — adds a third hole for **autolock mode** (direct belay on the anchor for bringing up a second).
- **Petzl Reverso** — similar to the ATC Guide.

A standard tube belay device weighs ~4 oz, accommodates rope 8.5–11 mm, and doubles as a rappel device (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 163).

### Assisted-braking (Grigri, Cinch)
The **Petzl Grigri** uses a cam-operated lock: under sudden load, the cam pinches the rope. Excellent for hangdog projecting and for novice belayers. Important caveats (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 165):

- **Do not use on trad climbs** (the manufacturer says so — the device locks on the slack side and can prematurely arrest rope feed).
- **Do not rappel** with a Grigri (single-strand only; doesn't accept a doubled rope).
- The brake hand still never leaves the rope.

### Munter hitch
A friction hitch tied directly into a pear-shaped locking biner. Replaces a device if one is dropped. Kinks the rope; popular in Europe. The action is good, the setup instantaneous — but the rope kinks substantially over time (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 165–166).

### Sticht plate
The original belay device (early 1970s) — a flat aluminum disc. Now obsolete; modern tube devices feed rope more smoothly (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 163).

### Figure-eight descender
Mainly a rappel device. The smaller hole accepts a bight of rope for belaying, but friction is marginal for a leader belay. *Not advised* for leader belaying (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 165).

## Using a belay device

1. Pass a bight of rope through the slot.
2. Clip a locking carabiner through the bight, the keeper-loop, *and* the belay loop on the harness.
3. Lock the biner. **Always.**
4. Brake-hand grip the rope on the side that bends sharply against the device (the "brake side").
5. Feed slack out with the guide hand, take in with the three-part sequence.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 160–161)

## Three-part take-in sequence

1. **Pull** with the guide hand from above the device, while the brake hand pulls down from below.
2. **Pinch** — guide hand crosses over and grips both rope strands below the device, freeing the brake hand.
3. **Slide** — brake hand slides back up to the device. Repeat.

The brake hand **never leaves the rope**.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 167–168)

## Direct vs. redirected belay

When belaying up a second from the top of a pitch (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 233–234):
- **Redirected belay** — device on harness; rope runs up through a biner on the anchor and back to the climber. Doubles the load through the pulley effect, but the belayer feels comfortable.
- **Direct belay** — device clipped *directly* to the anchor (Reverso / ATC Guide in autolock mode). Anchor takes the bulk of the load, not the belayer. Preferred by guides; standard in Europe.

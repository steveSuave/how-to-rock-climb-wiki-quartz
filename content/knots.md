## Tie-in to harness

### Figure-eight follow-through
The universal tie-in. Tie a single figure-eight in the rope, thread the tail through both harness tie-in points (the belay loop is not the tie-in point), and re-trace the figure-eight back through itself. Leaves a tail of ~6 inches. Easy to inspect ("two strands following each other through the entire knot"). Hard to untie after a fall — that's a feature, not a bug (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 100, 102).

### Double bowline
An alternative tie-in. Easier to untie after a fall, but the bowline must be backed up with a stopper knot. Less common in the US.

## Anchor and clip-in knots

### Figure-eight on a bight
A figure-eight tied in a doubled section of rope, producing a fixed loop. Clipped to a [[carabiners|locking biner]] at the power point. The standard tie-in to a [[belay-anchor]] (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 154–155).

### Overhand on a bight
A simpler, smaller version of the figure-eight on a bight. Used to back up other knots and to form the power point of a [[cordelette]] (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 155).

### Clove hitch
A friction hitch tied directly into a locking carabiner. Quick to set and *adjustable* — slide rope through to change length. Used to tie into anchor pieces individually, especially when belaying from the anchor. Requires practice; one wrong wrap and it's a half-hitch (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 155–156).

### Bowline on a coil
Old-school tie-in around the waist when no harness is available. Largely obsolete (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 100).

## Joining knots

### Water knot (ring bend)
A doubled overhand tied in webbing. The *only* knot to use for joining webbing. Tails ≥ 2 inches. Inspect and dress regularly (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 96).

### Double fisherman's (grapevine)
Two stacked overhand knots, one on each rope end, pulled tight against each other. The standard for joining cord into loops and for tying two ropes for rappels. Tough to untie after loading.

### Triple fisherman's
Three wraps per side instead of two. Required for Spectra/Dyneema/high-tensile cord (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 98).

### Double overhand (flat overhand, "EDK")
Both rope ends tied through an overhand together. Smallest knot profile of any rope-join — least likely to snag on rappel retrieval. Favored by experienced climbers; not for ropes of very different diameters (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 258).

### Figure-eight follow-through (for joining ropes)
Tie a figure-eight in one rope end and trace it back with the other rope. Backed up by a grapevine. The most secure rappel rope join.

### Square knot backed by grapevines
Easy to untie after loading; the grapevines prevent the square from rolling. Less compact than the EDK.

## Friction hitches

### Prusik
A loop of 6–7 mm cord wrapped three times around a thicker rope. Grips when loaded, slides when held. Standard for ascending a fixed line, backing up a rappel, or self-rescue (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 254).

### Klemheist (Autoblock)
Similar to Prusik but tied differently — releases under direct weight, used as a rappel backup clipped to the leg loop.

### Bachmann
Uses a carabiner as a handle alongside the cord wraps. Works with flat webbing (Prusiks need round cord) (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 254).

## Slinging hitches

### Girth hitch
Pass a sling around an object and through itself, cinching it down. The standard method for slinging trees, horns, and blocks at [[natural-anchors|natural anchors]] (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 108–110, Appendix B glossary).

## Belay hitches

### Munter hitch
A friction hitch tied directly into a large pear-shaped locking biner. Replaces a [[belay-device]] if one is dropped. Kinks the rope; popular in Europe, less so in the US (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 165–166).

## Stopper knot

A figure-eight or double overhand tied in the rope end before a rappel — prevents rappelling off the end of the rope. Standard practice in bad weather, dark, or fatigue (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 260).

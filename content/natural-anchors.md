## Why use natural anchors first

When a tree, block, or horn is available, it is usually stronger than any hand-placed protection and dispenses with the need to engineer a [[rigging-systems|multi-piece anchor]]. The book's rule: "Always go with an indisputable natural anchor whenever possible" (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 255).

## The natural anchors

### Trees
A live tree ≥ 6 inches in diameter and well-rooted is bomber. Sling it with a [[knots|girth hitch]] using webbing or a sewn sling. For rappel anchors, run the rope through *slings* with [[carabiners|rap rings]] — never directly around the bark (the rope can damage the tree and abrade the sheath) (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 108–110, 256).

### Blocks
Detached blocks of rock that sit on a ledge. Test by:
1. Looking at the contact surface — is the block sitting in a cup, or balanced on edge?
2. Pushing — does it move at all?
3. Thumping it — a hollow sound is bad.

Sling it like a tree (girth-hitch) or thread a sling around it.

### Horns / spikes
A flake-like projection of rock. Loop a sling over it. The horn must be:
- **Solid** — not a hollow flake.
- **Oriented correctly** — the sling must not lift off under load.

### Flakes
Sheets of rock attached to the wall. A flake with a thin top edge can be threaded with a sling (a "threader") or used for a chock placement behind it. Test the flake first — flakes break.

### Chockstones
A rock wedged in a crack. Often used for slinging (thread a sling behind it) or for [[passive-protection|nut placements]] above and below. Many chockstones are fragile — test before trusting (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 115–116).

### Threaded tunnels
A natural tunnel through the rock that a sling can be threaded through. Strong if the rock around the tunnel is solid. Common in limestone and tuff.

## Equalizing natural anchors

A single natural anchor can be the entire anchor *if* it's truly bomber (a thick living tree, a hulking block). Otherwise, link two naturals or a natural plus a [[passive-protection|nut]] using a [[rigging-systems|cordelette or sliding X]].

## Bombproof vs. "good enough"

"Bombproof" is reserved for things that would hold a cement truck — a 12" tree, a granite spike weighing tons. "Good enough" is the standard for [[belay-anchor|belay anchors]]: redundant, equalized, capable of holding a [[factor-2-fall|factor 2 fall]] (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 172).

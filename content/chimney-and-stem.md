## Squeeze chimneys

The width between "too-wide for off-width" and "comfortable chimney." Body cammed sideways: chest and back against the walls, knees and hips wedged. Progress is by alternating which body part takes weight while the others reset (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 70–73).

Some climbers like to be turned shoulder-to-rock; others prefer face-to-rock. Most of all, climbers hate squeeze chimneys.

## Chimneys (full body width)

Three primary positions, depending on width:

### Knee chimney
Knees against one wall, feet against the other. Climber sits in compression and rises by alternately weighting and unweighting the back and the legs (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 73–75).

### Bridge / stem chimney
Hands and feet on opposite walls; the back is *not* in contact. Used in wider chimneys where the climber can extend across. Less secure than the knee chimney but faster.

### Full-body stem
The widest variant: arms and legs spanning the walls; climber spread-eagled. Demands flexibility.

## Stemming

In a corner / dihedral wider than a body chimney, the climber bridges between the two faces — feet pushing outward against each wall, hands on whatever holds the corner offers. Stemming is friction-driven; smearing footwork dominates.

Stemming is the basic technique on dihedral routes — corner systems in granite (Yosemite) and limestone (Verdon Gorge).

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 80–83)

## Entry and exit

The hardest moves in a chimney are usually *getting in* and *getting out*. Going in means committing to the wide section before friction takes hold. Coming out means making a delicate transition from full-body compression to standard face or crack moves — often on small holds at the chimney's lip.

## Gear

Cams (largest sizes), [[passive-protection|Big Bros]], or threaded chockstones at the back of the chimney. Long runners are essential — the chimney often opens onto an exit move where the leader is well away from the crack itself.

## Anatomy of a sit harness

- **Waist belt** — padded; double-back the buckle (most modern harnesses auto double-back).
- **Leg loops** — padded; fixed or adjustable.
- **Belay loop** — the most-loaded loop on the harness. Strength-rated > 15 kN. **Only** the [[belay-device]] and rappel device clip here; the rope ties in through the waist belt and leg loops directly.
- **Gear loops** — non-load-bearing plastic loops for racking gear.
- **Haul loop** (rear) — non-load-bearing tag-line attachment.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 103–105)

## Tying in

Standard tie-in: **figure-eight follow-through** threaded through both the waist belt loop and the leg loop tie-in points — *not* through the belay loop. This makes the harness the redundant element: even if one waist webbing fails, the leg loops hold.

The Todd Skinner fatality at Leaning Tower (Yosemite) — a worn-out belay loop on a too-old harness — is cited by the book as the reason to retire harnesses well before they show severe wear (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 265).

## Buckling check

Always double-back the buckle. Many fatalities have started with an unfinished buckle (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 105).

The **BARK** check before any rappel includes the **B**uckle:
- **B**uckle
- **A**nchor
- **R**appel device
- **K**not

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 255)

## Retirement

Retire a harness when:
- Belay loop, tie-in points, or buckle webbing show fraying or fuzz exposing internal threads.
- Continuous use ≥ 3–5 years.
- After a major fall.
- After exposure to chemicals (battery acid, harsh solvents).

A new harness is cheap; the body inside it is not (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 265).

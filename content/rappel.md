## Why rappel is dangerous

A rappeller is continuously stressing every link in the safety chain — anchor, knot, harness, device, biner — under full body weight, often *with no backup*. If any link fails, the result is final. The vast majority of rappel accidents are human error: anchor failure (untested), knot failure (untied or wrong), rappelling off the end of the rope (no stopper knot) (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 250).

## Pre-rappel check: BARK

Before leaning back, double-check (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 255):
- **B**uckle — harness buckle properly doubled-back.
- **A**nchor — bombproof and properly rigged.
- **R**appel device — clipped through both rope strands and a locking biner on the belay loop.
- **K**not — joining the two rope ends, properly tied.

## Body position

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 251)
- Body at ~45° to the wall.
- Legs extended, knees bent.
- Feet wide apart for stability.
- Brake hand near the hip.
- Upper torso bent slightly toward the wall; head turned to look down past the brake hand.

Walk down. Don't bound — sudden stops shock-load the anchor and heat the rope.

## Hands

| Hand | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Brake hand** | Controls speed by pulling the rope into the device. Never leaves the rope. |
| **Guide hand** | Balance only. Loosely cups the rope above the device. |

## Brake-hand backups

A friction knot on the rope below the device, clipped to the leg loop. If the rappeller loses control, the knot locks (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 252–254).

- **Prusik** — 6–7 mm cord, triple-wrap. Easy, well-tested. Standard choice.
- **Autoblock (Klemheist)** — uses a sling; releases under direct weight.
- **Bachmann** — uses a carabiner as a handle; works with webbing.

A backup is mandatory for novices and good practice for everyone. Many guides require an Autoblock on every rappel.

## Rappel anchors

Always **two bombproof points minimum**, SRENE. Never lower or rappel through:
- A single bolt.
- A single nut.
- Aluminum rap rings (only the rope passes through these on the *very last* rappel, never the device).
- Old, fuzzy nylon slings.

Add a sling or two if existing slings look suspect (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 255–257).

## Joining rappel ropes

| Knot | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Double overhand (EDK / flat overhand)** | Smallest profile, least snag-prone. Favored. Don't use with mismatched diameters. |
| **Figure-eight follow-through + grapevine backup** | Most secure, easier to untie. |
| **Double fisherman's** | Old standard; jams after loading. |
| **Square knot + grapevines** | Easier to untie than double fisherman's, but kinks the rope. |

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 258–259)

## Setting the rope

- Throw rope ends down on a quarter-coil, top first.
- Rappel side of the rope: the strand that goes *through* the knot first. (Pulling the correct strand retrieves the rope.)
- Tie a **stopper knot** in each end — figure-eight or double overhand. Prevents rappelling off the end (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 260).
- Inspect the line of descent — no loose rock, sharp edges, or snag points.

## Stuck ropes

The most tense moment of a multi-rappel descent. Avoid stuck ropes (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 266):

- Set the join *below* the lip / first crack so the knot doesn't get pulled into a constriction.
- Last person down checks for snags during descent.
- The knot side of the rope should run on the side of the *thinner* anchor sling.
- Don't pull from below a snag — falling rock above the snag can hit you.

If the rope is stuck and the climber is on the ground, options range from a hard jerk, to climbing back up the rope on Prusiks, to abandoning the rope. Always have a backup plan.

## Hanging transfers

The crux of multi-rappel descents on overhanging walls. At each station, clip into the anchor *independently of the rope*, only then unclip from the rappel device. The first person down rigs the next rappel before unclipping (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 267).

## Last-resort methods

- **Carabiner brake rappel** — if the device is dropped: 4 biners (2 platform, 2 brake) with gates opposed. Cumbersome but works (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 260, 262).
- **Body rappel (dulfersitz)** — no devices, no carabiners; rope wrapped around the body. Painful, last-resort only.

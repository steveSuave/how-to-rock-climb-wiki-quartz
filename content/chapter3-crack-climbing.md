## Why cracks are different

Face holds are obvious; cracks force the climber to *manufacture* a hold by camming the body into a fracture. Jamming is counterintuitive at first and uses muscles a face climber rarely trains (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 49). Cracks are also where most natural [[passive-protection]] sits, so leading them well is the gateway to trad climbing.

## Crack sizes and the technique that fits

| Size | Body part jammed | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tips | Fingertip / pinky lock | First knuckle in; thumb-up or thumb-down |
| Fingers | Finger jam | Cammed by twisting |
| Off-fingers | Thumb stack / butterfly | Awkward width between fingers and hand |
| Hand | Hand jam (tripod) | The most secure jam; cupped or cammed |
| Fist | Fist jam | Knuckles in, thumb tucked |
| Off-width | Arm bar, chicken wing, heel-toe, T-bar, Leavittation | Hardest jamming there is |
| Squeeze chimney | Knees + back + hips | Too wide to body-cam, too narrow to bridge |
| Chimney | Stem, bridge, full-body | Body-width fissure |

See [[jamming]], [[off-width-techniques]], and [[chimney-and-stem]] for details.

## Lieback (Frankenstein)

When a crack is a corner or a vertical fin, climbers can pull sideways on its edge while pushing feet against the opposing wall. The [[lieback]] is fast but exhausting — protection is awkward to place and rest is impossible (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 76–80).

## Taping

A taped hand turns thin skin into a glove that resists the grinding of jams. The standard tape job wraps the back of the hand and the base of each finger, leaving the palm bare (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 84–85).

## Shoes for cracks

Stiffer shoes (or board-lasted slip-lasted slippers) protect the foot in thin cracks; soft shoes are more painful and harder to torque (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 83).

## The shift

Seventy-five years ago: the leader must never fall — gear was unreliable, ropes broke. Today: controlled falling is standard practice on sport routes and even on safe trad climbs. Many leading climbers don't feel they've pushed themselves until they've logged some "air time" (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 228).

## What makes a fall safe vs. dangerous

A fall is safe when:
- The protection above is bomber.
- Nothing below to hit (no ledge, no flake, no slab decking out potential).
- The belayer is alert and anchored.
- The rope is between the leader's legs in a straight line.

A fall is dangerous when:
- Pro is sketchy and may zipper.
- Below: a ledge, slab, talus.
- Belayer is distracted or far off line.
- Rope is behind a leg (the falling body flips upside down).
- A sharp edge of rock could cut the rope.

## Body position in a fall

| Terrain | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Slab | Slide down on feet, palms pressing off, chest and head far from the surface. |
| Vertical | Legs extended and slightly bent, arms in front, eyes on the rock. |
| Overhanging | Stay relaxed, arms and feet in front of you, absorb impact on landing into the rope. |
| Traverse with swing | Catlike posture, knees bent, arms forward. |

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 228–229)

**Rules:**
- **Stay relaxed.** A rigid body is more likely to break.
- **Face the rock.** Don't tumble.
- **Don't freeze, don't go limp.** Neither extreme is right.
- **Bent knees as shock absorbers.**

## Anticipating the fall

Often you know you're going to fall before you do — fingers buttering, strength fading, pump rising. Warn the belayer ("FALLING!" or just "WATCH ME"), and don't panic. A controlled fall is far safer than an off-the-rails surprise (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 230).

## After a long fall

A long fall (20+ ft) elongates the rope's fibers temporarily. Lower down, tie off the anchor with slings, and wait ~10 minutes for the rope to recover its stretch before re-leading (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 230).

## Pendulum / swing falls

The most dangerous fall pattern: traversing leader falls and swings into the line of pro. Slamming into a corner from 20 ft to the side is the equivalent of falling 20 ft straight onto a ledge (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 228). The defense: place pro that protects against the swing — leader for traverses should plug pro often.

## Falling onto the belay (factor 2)

The worst-case fall. The leader falls *before placing the first piece* off the [[belay-anchor]] — see [[factor-2-fall]] and [[jesus-nut]]. Forces are highest, the belayer cannot effectively help, the consequences may be lethal. The defense: build a bomber anchor, place the [[jesus-nut]] *immediately* off the belay.

## Lead falls vs. toprope falls

A leader fall is *twice the rope-out distance* (out and back). A toprope fall is just the rope stretch — typically inches.

A short fall on little rope ("wrencher" — 8–10 ft) is brutal: very little rope means very little stretch, very high impact force. Most climbers underestimate these (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 228).

## Practice

Get comfortable falling on safe sport routes. Take small lead falls intentionally to recalibrate fear. A pokey climber is almost always afraid; toprope the route first, then lead it (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 227).

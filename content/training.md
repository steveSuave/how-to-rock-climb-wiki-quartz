## What climbing trains

- **Fingers and forearms** — strengthened through sinew, not muscle. Climb directly on holds.
- **Lats** (latissimus dorsi) — prime movers in the pull-up motion. Build with climbing and weighted pull-ups.
- **Antagonists** (chest, trapezius, abdominals) — climbing alone neglects them. Must be balanced or injury follows (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 283).
- **Core** — power delivery comes from hip and torso. See [[face-climbing]].

## Recovery rule

Any muscle trained to failure needs **48 hours** to recover. Hard workouts every third day beat daily mediocrity (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 284).

## Climbing as training

The best training for climbing is climbing. Mileage wires technique, builds endurance, and refines movement. [[bouldering|Bouldering]] is unmatched for raw power.

## Hangboard

Wall-mounted resin or wood board with a variety of holds — jugs, edges, pockets, slopers. Introduced in 1986. Use:

- **6–10 laps** of 5–7 grip positions per session.
- **5-minute rests** between burns.
- Heavy days *every third day*, not daily.
- Add weight (waist-mounted) to keep load on the fingers instead of the shoulders.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 292–293)

The shoulder is the hangboard's main injury point — at full hang the body weight sags through the rotator-cuff tendons. Don't hang to failure on small holds without weight.

## Campus board

A plywood sheet with parallel wood rungs, screwed every 4–8 inches in a 15–20° overhanging ladder. Climber moves rung-to-rung **with hands only** (no feet) — explosive contact strength training (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 292–293).

Three or four sessions per week is plenty. Don't start campus boarding without months of solid climbing background — tendon injuries are common.

## Weight training

A 5-lb gain in strength can outweigh a 5-lb gain in body mass. Climbing-relevant lifts:
- **Pull-ups** (weighted).
- **Lat work** — lat pulldowns, rows.
- **Antagonists** — chest press, shoulder press.
- **Core** — hanging leg raises, planks.

Stretch between sets. The book's author has trained with weights for 35+ years and never had an injury that kept him out of the gym for more than a week (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 296).

## Freehand exercises

Push-ups, pull-ups, sit-ups, dips. Calisthenics. Less injury-risk than weights, less productive at building peak strength.

## Stretching

Yoga, sport-specific stretches. Flexibility enables high steps and drop knees on steep rock. Often climbed but rarely trained (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 294).

## Cardio

Strong heart = faster recovery = more tissue repair. Best climbing cardio:
- **Jump rope** — skill-rich, full-body, lung-builder. The book's recommended gold standard.
- Running, cycling, basketball, swimming.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 296)

## Injury

Tendon injuries are the climbing-specific concern. Dr. Mark Robinson's recovery protocol (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 297):

1. Decrease activity until pain and swelling are gone.
2. Wait two more weeks.
3. Easy strength exercises (putty, gum) for 2–3 weeks.
4. Low-angle climbing on big holds for a month.
5. High-angle climbing on big holds for a month.
6. Back to full bore.

Anti-inflammatories (ibuprofen, aspirin) control symptoms but must not mask pain so you can overuse. Glucosamine and chondroitin help.

## Injury prevention

- **Warm up.** Ten minutes on easy terrain before pushing.
- **Tape** weak fingers around either side of the second joint.
- **Don't push through** sharp pain.
- **Stop before you make it worse.**

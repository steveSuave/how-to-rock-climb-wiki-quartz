## What climbing trains

- **Fingers and forearms** — sinew, not muscle. Trained directly on holds.
- **Lats** — prime movers in pull-up motion.
- **Antagonists** — chest, trapezius, abdominals — must be balanced or injury follows (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 283).
- **Recovery** — *any* muscle trained to failure needs 48 hours to recover. Hard workouts every third day beat daily mediocrity.

The best training for climbing is climbing itself. For raw power, bouldering and campus boards. For general conditioning, weights. For technique, mileage.

## Bouldering

[[bouldering|Bouldering]] is roped climbing's training ground and a serious sport in its own right (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 284). Highball bouldering is essentially free soloing — beginners stay below jump-off height. Always use a crash pad and a spotter; clear the landing.

## Toproping

A rope from above. The mainstay of beginner climbing and a great way to wire a difficult sport route in preparation for the redpoint. Setup involves rigging a [[belay-anchor]] over the lip of the cliff, extending it just over the edge to keep the rope from running over the lip (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 286–289). See [[toprope]].

If the belayer is much lighter than the climber, anchor the belayer down. A swinging fall onto a poorly placed [[toprope]] anchor is the source of many accidents.

## Climbing gyms

Indoor gyms have democratized training: predictable weather, customized problems, route-setting as an art form. The book points out one transition trap: gym-trained climbers who can't yet handle the unpredictability of outdoor rock — protection, weather, rockfall, route-finding (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 292). Go with an experienced friend or a certified guide for the first outdoor trips. See [[climbing-gym]].

## Hangboards and campus boards

- **Hangboards** (introduced 1986) — wall-mounted resin or wood boards with a variety of holds. Six to ten laps with five-minute rests; volume is low, intensity is high (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 292–293).
- **Campus boards** — overhanging plywood with parallel wood rungs. Climber moves rung-to-rung *with hands only*. Builds explosive contact strength. Three or four sessions a week is plenty.

Always warm up. Hangboard injuries are usually at the *shoulders* (tendons sag at full hang) — adding weight at the waist keeps the body weight on the fingers rather than the shoulders.

## Weight training

A 5-lb gain in strength can outweigh a 5-lb gain in body mass. Climbing-relevant lifts: pull-ups, lat work, antagonists, core. Stretch between sets. The book's author claims weight training has *never* caused him an injury that kept him out of the gym for more than a week (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 296).

## Stretching

Yoga, sport-specific stretches. Flexibility enables high steps, side stems, and keeping hips near steep rock. Often climbed but rarely trained (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 294).

## Cardiovascular

Strong heart = faster recovery = more tissue repair. Jump rope is the gold standard (skill-rich, full-body, lung-builder). Running, cycling, basketball all work (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 296).

## Injuries and prevention

Sport climbing's emphasis on overhanging routes makes tendon and ligament sprains common. Dr. Mark Robinson's recovery protocol (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 297):

1. Decrease activity until pain and swelling are gone.
2. Wait two more weeks.
3. Easy strength exercises (putty, gum) for 2–3 weeks.
4. Low-angle climbing on big holds for a month.
5. High-angle climbing on big holds for a month.
6. Back to full bore.

Anti-inflammatories control symptoms but must not be used to mask pain so you can overuse. Tape weak fingers around either side of the second joint. Glucosamine and chondroitin help.

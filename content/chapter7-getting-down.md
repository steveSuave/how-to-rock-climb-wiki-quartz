## Descent is dangerous

A disproportionate share of climbing accidents happen on the way down — fatigue, loss of concentration, and skipped safety checks (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 247). [[rappel|Rappelling]] is climbing's second most dangerous activity after leader falls.

## Options

1. **Walk off** — usually preferred when easy and short.
2. **Downclimbing** — competent downclimbing is essential to alpine and adventure climbing; cross-stepping is more efficient than shuffling (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 248–249).
3. **Rappel** — fastest but consequential. Always *plan the descent before going up*.

## Rappel basics

- Body at ~45° to the wall, knees bent, feet wide, brake hand at hip.
- Guide hand never death-grips — it can't stop you.
- Brake hand never leaves the rope.
- Smooth and steady, no bouncing — sudden stops shock-load the system.
- Always tie stopper knots in the rope ends.
- Always inspect every component before leaning back: **BARK** = Buckle, Anchor, Rappel device, Knot (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 255).

## Brake-hand backups

A friction knot below the device, clipped to the leg loop, locks if you let go. Standard knots:
- **Prusik** — accessory cord (7–8 mm) tied with a triple wrap. Easy, well-tested.
- **Autoblock (Klemheist)** — works with a sling or cord; releases under direct weight.
- **Bachmann** — uses a carabiner as a handle; works with flat webbing.

See [[rappel]] for tying details.

## Rappel anchors

A minimum of two bombproof points, SRENE, never lower or rappel through aluminum rap rings or a single piece. When using a tree, run the rope through slings and rap rings — never directly around the bark. Cold shuts and chain links are standard at modern bolted stations (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 255–256).

## Knots for joining rappel ropes

- **Double overhand (EDK / flat overhand)** — smallest profile, least likely to snag. Favored by experienced climbers. Don't use with very different rope diameters.
- **Figure-eight follow-through with grapevine backup** — most secure, easier to untie after weighting.
- **Double fisherman's** — old standard; tough to untie.
- **Square knot backed by grapevines** — works but kinks the rope.

(source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 258–259)

## Stuck lines

Survey the terrain below for snag hazards before tossing the rope. The first one down tests the pull. If a knot sticks, options range from a sturdy jerk to climbing back up on Prusiks to leaving the rope. *Never* pull from below a snag in your rope — falling rock above the snag could hit you (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 266).

## Hanging transfers

The crux of multi-rappel descents on overhanging walls: arrive at the next anchor, clip in *independently of the rope*, only then unclip from the rappel device. The first person down rigs the next rappel; the last person feeds the rope through (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 267).

## Carabiner brake and body rappel

If you drop your rappel device, two pairs of opposed locking biners with two more biners as the brake works (the **carabiner brake**). If you have *no gear*, the **body rappel (dulfersitz)** wraps the rope around your body — painful, last-resort only (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, pp. 262–264).

## Coiling the rope

Standing coil, mountaineer's (butterfly) coil, or stuff into a rope bag. The butterfly coil produces fewer kinks and lets the climber carry the rope with arms free (source: how-to-rock-climb.pdf, p. 269).
